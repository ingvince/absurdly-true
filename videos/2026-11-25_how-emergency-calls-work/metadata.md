## Facts (verified)
- 911/112 calls are routed to the PSAP covering the caller's area (Enhanced 911, Wikipedia; Dauphin County 911).
- Advanced Mobile Location: the phone switches on GPS/Wi-Fi for ~20 s and sends its location; radius 50 m or less for ~85% of calls (EENA AML FAQ, 2018).
- Call-taker asks location and nature of emergency; can stay on the line giving instructions while help is sent (Dauphin County); telecommunicator CPR (American Heart Association).
- No own-carrier coverage: emergency roaming through another operator's network (SOS Alarm, Sweden 112; ScienceABC); iPhone shows "SOS" in the status bar (TidBITS).
- No network: Emergency SOS via satellite on iPhone 14 and later (Apple) and Pixel 9 and later except 9a (Google), in supported countries, outdoors with a clear sky; location is sent.
- 999: London, 30 June 1937, the world's oldest emergency call service (Wikipedia).

## Script (155 words, MiniMax "Decent Young Man", 64.10 s, 1923 frames)
Someone asked me how emergency calls actually work. Here's the whole trip. You dial nine-one-one, or one-one-two in Europe. Your call goes straight to the emergency center covering your area. On many smartphones, your phone quietly sends its GPS location, often within fifty meters. A call-taker asks where you are and what happened. Help can be on the way while you're still talking. They can even coach you through CPR. And when there's no signal? Look at the top of your screen. If it says SOS, or emergency calls only, your own carrier can't reach you, but your phone can borrow the nearest tower of another carrier, just for the emergency call. So always try calling. No network at all? Newer iPhones and Pixels can text emergency services through a satellite. Step outside, point your phone at the sky, and it sends your location too. And the very first emergency number? Nine-nine-nine. London, nineteen thirty-seven.
